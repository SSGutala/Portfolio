Overview

LilPreneur is a story-driven business simulator for kids ages 7 to 12. Parents load a virtual wallet. Kids run a business built from the true stories of real young founders, make every call themselves, and earn badges. Parents then get a report on each choice, how their kid thinks, and what to do next.

Kids never talk to a chatbot or to other kids. That was the number one request across our 40+ parent and educator interviews.

Customer's Challenge

Three in four kids want to be their own boss (76% of 8 to 14 year olds in Visa research on Gen Alpha, UK, 2023). They get worksheets, or games with no stakes. We call it the aspiration gap: big ambition, met with abstract theory instead of real decisions. Kids have no safe place to practice. Parents see hours of screen time and no idea what is being learned. Teachers have finance mandates and few hands-on tools for the early grades. Where school does teach money, it comes late. Thirty states now require a personal finance course to graduate, usually taken at 16 or 17, and entrepreneurship is mostly an elective. Very little of it reaches a 9 year old, and almost none of it involves running something.

So the kids who figure it out early figure it out alone. Alina Morse asked at 7 why candy could not be good for teeth and was running Zolli Candy on $6M in retail sales by 13. Mikaila Ulmer started a lemonade stand at 4 that became a brand sold in Whole Foods. Moziah Bridges signed an NBA licensing deal at 15. None of them learned pricing in a classroom.

To understand our users' pain points, goals, and motivations, we ran 40+ voice of customer interviews with parents and educators.

What did we ask?

  • Parent, teacher, and child background
  • What their child already does with money
  • How they find and choose learning apps
  • What they pay for today, and how
  • What proof of learning they expect
  • Safety and data concerns with apps for kids
  • Top pain points

The biggest thing we learned: parents do not want their kids talking to chatbots or to other kids. It was the number one request. They also want visible evidence that their child is learning, which is why the parent report sits at the center of the product.

Feedback before Beta

Reviewing the spec, the flow chart, and the designs side by side surfaced 55 gaps, 9 of them blocking. The ones that changed the product:

  • The suggested price label gave away the answer, so the pricing screen was not a real decision.
  • Every designed outcome was a win. There was no screen for losing money.
  • No parental consent screen existed, and the parent PIN had no way to be set.
  • Partnering cost half the earnings and gave nothing back, so no kid should ever choose it.
This is feedback from the internal review. Once beta is running, add completion rate, replay rate, and what kids and parents said.

User Stories and Acceptance Criteria

Running the Stand
$
As a young entrepreneur, I want to pick the price of my lollipops, so that I can see how price changes what I sell and earn.
The app should offer three price options and require one before continuing. The same choice should always produce the same outcome.
!
As a young entrepreneur, I want a heads up when my spot is weak, so that I can change my mind before the day starts.
The app should show a nudge with two options on any spot except the busiest. The child should never interact with AI directly.
?
As a young entrepreneur, I want to talk to a customer, so that I learn how my answer can win or lose a sale.
The app should offer two responses with different outcomes, and count the result in the day end numbers.
=
As a young entrepreneur, I want to see what I made at the end of the day, so that I know if my choices worked.
The app should show made, sold, price, revenue, expenses, and profit in whole dollars, read aloud.
Parent Experience
R
As a parent, I want a report after each session, so that I can see what my child decided and what it taught.
The report should list each decision, its outcome, and the concept behind it. Opening it twice should show the same content.
C
As a parent, I want to give consent and control my child's data, so that I can trust the product.
The app should collect no child data before consent is recorded. Deleting a child profile should remove every record tied to that child.
T
As a parent, I want to set time limits, so that play fits our house rules.
The limit should be enforced on the server and should let the child finish the current selling day.
App Performance
S
As a parent, I want settings locked behind my PIN, so that my child cannot change them.
The app should refuse to return settings without a valid PIN, checked on the server.
W
As a kid on weak wifi, I want my choices saved once, so that a double tap does not break my results.
The app should save each decision as it happens and ignore duplicates.
A
As a kid who is still learning to read, I want every decision explained out loud, so that I can play on my own.
The app should play narration on every decision screen, from pre-recorded audio.

Risks and Remediations

Schedule Risk: engine work starts before the game constants are final and gets built twice.
Start with accounts, consent, and the data model. Hold the engine until constants are corrected.
Budget Risk: voice input and live text to speech add servers and per-play cost.
Cut voice input. Narration and art are pre-recorded, with no live generation.
Operational Risk: the spec, the flow chart, and the designs describe different games.
One gap review across all three. The designs became the source of truth for Level 1.
Technical Risk: the simulation math drifts and quietly corrupts every report.
Versioned constants and a fixed set of test playthroughs on every commit.
Programmatic Risk: COPPA requires verifiable parental consent before collecting data from a child under 13.
Consent before any child data, a written retention policy, full deletion on request, and written permission for founder stories and photos.
Business Risk: kids play once and parents do not convert.
Free pilot first. Open the paywall only after kids finish and parents value the report.

Pricing Strategy

Families
  • Beta is free, starting October 20, 2026, with about 80 families projected.
  • Phase 1 is $5 a month, with six businesses (three physical, three digital). Premium unlocks higher levels.
  • Phase 2 is $10 a month, where kids combine products and sell them in one stand.
  • For reference, families already pay BusyKid $4, Greenlight from $5.99, and Acorns Early $8 a month.
Schools
  • $500 per classroom per year. The teacher brings it into class, kids bring it home, and the parent subscribes.
Target
  • 240K families (about 1% of roughly 24M US kids ages 7 to 12) at $5 to $10 a month, plus 10K classrooms: $19M to $34M in annual recurring revenue. These are projections, not booked revenue.
